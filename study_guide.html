Q 1
How can one describe a natural or social system for modeling & simulation? How are time and space handled? Why do we need various approaches? What are boundary and initial conditions?
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Before you can simulate anything you must decide what to keep and what to throw away. A model is a deliberately simplified cartoon of reality: you pick the state variables, decide how time ticks and how space is laid out, and state what happens at the edges (boundary conditions) and at the start (initial condition). Different questions need different cartoons — that's why no single framework fits everything.

What is a Model?

A model is a simplified, abstract representation of a real system that captures the essential features relevant to the question being asked. The goal is NOT perfect replication — it is to understand a specific behavior or phenomenon.

  • Real system → abstraction → model → simulation → results → insight into real system
  • "All models are wrong, but some are useful" (Box)

Describing a System

A system is described by:

  • State variables: quantities that define the system at any point in time (e.g., temperature, position, population)
  • Parameters: fixed values that characterize the system (e.g., viscosity, birth rate)
  • Interactions/Rules: how components affect each other

MesoIntermediateFluid parcels, groups of agents

How is TIME Handled?

The choice of how to represent time shapes the entire mathematical framework of a model. There are two fundamental options:

  • Continuous time (t ∈ ℝ⁺): The state evolves smoothly and is described by differential equations (ODEs or PDEs). The system can be queried at any instant. Example: dx/dt = f(x, t). Appropriate when the underlying physics is smooth — fluid flow, population growth, chemical reactions.
  • Discrete time (t ∈ {0, Δt, 2Δt, ...}): The state advances in fixed steps and is described by difference equations or maps. Example: x(n+1) = f(x(n)). Natural for computer simulation and for systems that update at regular intervals (generations in ecology, daily infection counts in epidemics).
⚠ Key Warning
Discrete Event Simulation has CONTINUOUS time — it jumps between events, not fixed steps.

How is SPACE Handled?

Space can be represented in several ways, each imposing different constraints on interactions and leading to qualitatively different emergent behaviors:

  • Continuous space: Positions are real-valued coordinates in ℝ² or ℝ³ — any point in the domain is accessible. Used in particle simulations (molecular dynamics, N-body gravity) and Boids. Interactions are typically defined by a cutoff radius or computed for all pairs.
  • Discrete space (grid/lattice): Space is divided into a regular array of cells, each with a discrete address (i, j). Used in cellular automata, the Lattice Boltzmann Method, and fire-spread models. The geometry of the lattice (square vs hexagonal) affects the isotropy of spreading patterns.
  • Network: Space is replaced entirely by graph connectivity — who can interact with whom is determined by the presence of an edge, not by geographic distance. The right representation for social dynamics (opinion spreading, epidemic on a contact network).
  • No space (lumped/mean-field): The system is treated as a single well-mixed container where every agent interacts equally with every other. ODE population models (SIR, Lotka-Volterra) implicitly assume this. Analytically tractable but ignores all spatial heterogeneity and clustering.

Why Do We Need VARIOUS Modeling Approaches?

No single modeling framework can capture all natural and social phenomena. Each approach is tailored to a specific scale, level of description, and type of question:

  • Particle methods: Track individual particles (positions and velocities) interacting pairwise — suited to N-body gravitational systems and molecular dynamics where microscopic detail matters.
  • Lattice Boltzmann: Uses a mesoscopic kinetic description to simulate fluid dynamics and recover the Navier-Stokes equations — ideal for complex geometries and multi-phase flows where the continuum PDE approach is hard to implement.
  • Dynamical systems / ODEs: Describe aggregated quantities (population size, infection count) evolving continuously in time — best when individual differences are unimportant and the system can be reduced to a few state variables.
  • Cellular automata / ABM: Encode simple local rules for individual agents and observe emergent global patterns — suited to social behavior and spatial dynamics where the macro-level behavior is not obvious from the micro-level rules.
  • Discrete Event Simulation: Advances time by jumping between events (arrivals, collisions) rather than fixed steps — used for queuing systems and collision-driven processes where most of the time nothing happens.
  • Network models: Replace geometric space with graph connectivity to study how the structure of social or physical networks shapes the spread of information, disease, or influence.

The choice of approach depends on the scale of interest (micro/meso/macro), whether behavior is deterministic or stochastic, computational cost constraints, and what observables are needed to answer the scientific question.

Initial Conditions

The initial condition is the complete state of the system at time t = 0: x(0) = x₀. Critical: small differences in IC can lead to vastly different outcomes (chaos).

Boundary Conditions

Boundary conditions specify what happens at the edges of the simulation domain. Without them, the problem is not well-posed.

Q 2
What is a dynamical system? Continuous vs discrete time. Fixed point, limit cycle, chaos. Examples.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
A dynamical system is just "a rule plus a starting point": tell me the state now and the rule tells me the state next. No matter how complicated, the long-term fate boils down to three destinies — settle to a point (fixed point), loop forever (limit cycle), or never repeat and depend hypersensitively on the start (chaos).

Definition

A dynamical system is a system whose state evolves over time according to a well-defined rule. Formally: the triple (T, X, Φ) where:

  • T = time domain (ℝ continuous, ℤ discrete)
  • X = state space (phase space)
  • Φ: T × X → X = evolution rule (flow or map)

Continuous Time — ODEs

In continuous time, the state x(t) evolves smoothly and is governed by an ordinary differential equation of the form dx/dt = f(x, t). The system is called autonomous if f does not depend explicitly on t — the dynamics look the same no matter when you observe them. Classic examples from this course:

dx/dt = f(x, t)   (autonomous if f doesn't depend on t)
  • Logistic growth: dN/dt = rN(1 − N/K) — population grows exponentially when N ≪ K and saturates as N approaches the carrying capacity K due to resource competition.
  • Lotka-Volterra: dx/dt = αx − βxy (prey grows at rate α but is consumed by predators at rate β·y); dy/dt = δxy − γy (predators grow by consuming prey at rate δ·x but die naturally at rate γ). Produces self-sustaining oscillations in both populations.
  • Lorenz system: dx/dt = σ(y−x); dy/dt = x(ρ−z)−y; dz/dt = xy−βz. A simplified model of atmospheric convection and the prototypical example of deterministic chaos — despite being fully deterministic, its trajectories are sensitive to initial conditions.

Discrete Time — Maps

In discrete time, the state advances in fixed steps via a map: x(n+1) = f(x(n)). Each application of f corresponds to one time step. The same nonlinear phenomena (fixed points, period doubling, chaos) appear in maps as in ODEs, but in a form that is directly iterable on a computer. The canonical example is the logistic map:

x(n+1) = r·x(n)·(1 − x(n))

Here r is the only parameter. For r < 3 the map converges to a stable fixed point; for 3 < r < 3.57 it undergoes a cascade of period doublings (2 → 4 → 8 → ...); and for r > 3.57 the behavior is chaotic — all of this complexity from a single quadratic equation.

Fixed Points (Equilibria)

A fixed point x* is a state where the system does not change:

  • Continuous: f(x*) = 0
  • Discrete: f(x*) = x*

Stability is determined by linearizing the dynamics around x* and examining the eigenvalues λ of the Jacobian matrix Df(x*). A small perturbation δx = x − x* evolves according to δẋ ≈ Df(x*)·δx, so the eigenvalues control whether perturbations grow or shrink:

  • Re(λ) < 0 for all eigenvalues → stable attractor: all perturbations decay exponentially back toward x*. The fixed point acts like a sink in phase space.
  • Re(λ) > 0 for any eigenvalue → unstable repellor: perturbations in the corresponding direction grow exponentially and the system moves away from x*. Acts like a source.
  • Re(λ) = 0 → center (neutrally stable): the linear analysis is inconclusive. Higher-order terms must be examined to determine stability (e.g., Lotka-Volterra fixed point is a center with neutrally stable cycles).

Limit Cycles

A limit cycle is a closed, isolated trajectory in phase space — the system oscillates periodically and neighboring trajectories spiral toward (stable) or away from (unstable) it. Example: Van der Pol oscillator → stable limit cycle. Lotka-Volterra → closed orbits (neutrally stable cycles).

Chaos

Chaos is deterministic but unpredictable behavior characterized by:

  • SDIC: two trajectories starting arbitrarily close diverge exponentially — "butterfly effect"
  • Bounded: not diverging to infinity
  • Aperiodic: never repeats exactly
  • Quantified by Lyapunov exponent λ > 0

Logistic map: r < 3 → fixed point; 3 < r < ~3.57 → period doubling; r > ~3.57 → chaos.

Q 3
Modeling a process via balance equation. Numerical methods to solve a dynamical system.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Almost every model in science is bookkeeping: the change of a quantity = what flows in − what flows out (+ sources − sinks). That balance is the differential equation. Since we can rarely solve it on paper, we march it forward in tiny steps on a computer (Euler, Runge–Kutta) — trading a little accuracy for the power to handle almost anything.

The Balance Equation

d(Q)/dt = (Rate IN) − (Rate OUT) + (Source) − (Sink)

This is the master template for all dynamical models. It embodies conservation laws. Steps: (1) identify quantity to track, (2) identify domain, (3) write fluxes in/out, (4) add source/sink terms.

Example: SIR Epidemic Model

dS/dt = -β·S·I/N        (outflow: infections)
dI/dt = β·S·I/N - γ·I   (inflow: infections; outflow: recovery)
dR/dt = γ·I              (inflow: recoveries)
Conservation: S + I + R = N = const

Example: Heat Diffusion (PDE)

ρc ∂T/∂t = k ∇²T   (heat equation)

Numerical Methods

We approximate x(t) at discrete steps t₀, t₁, t₂, ... with step size Δt.

Forward Euler (Explicit)

x(n+1) = x(n) + Δt · f(x(n), t(n))
  • Pros: Simple, O(1) per step
  • Cons: First-order accuracy (error ~ Δt), can be unstable if Δt too large
  • Stability condition: Δt < 2/|λ_max|

Backward Euler (Implicit)

x(n+1) = x(n) + Δt · f(x(n+1), t(n+1))
  • Pros: Unconditionally stable → can use large Δt
  • Cons: Must solve implicit equation at each step

Runge-Kutta 4th Order (RK4) — Gold Standard

k₁ = f(x(n), t(n))
k₂ = f(x(n) + Δt·k₁/2, t(n) + Δt/2)
k₃ = f(x(n) + Δt·k₂/2, t(n) + Δt/2)
k₄ = f(x(n) + Δt·k₃, t(n+1))

x(n+1) = x(n) + (Δt/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄)
  • Pros: 4th-order accuracy (error ~ Δt⁴), excellent accuracy/cost ratio
  • Cons: 4 function evaluations per step; still explicit

Q 4
Main quantities of a graph/network. Adjacency matrix. Small-world networks. Degree distribution.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
A network strips a system down to "who is connected to whom". Once you forget geography and keep only the wiring diagram, a handful of numbers — how many links each node has, how clustered they are, how far apart on average — capture surprisingly deep properties, like the six-degrees-of-separation small-world effect.

Basic Definitions

A graph G = (V, E) consists of V = vertices (nodes): |V| = N, and E = edges (links): |E| = L. Types: directed/undirected, weighted/unweighted.

Key Quantities

Degree: k_i = Σ_j A_ij (number of edges at node i). Average degree: ⟨k⟩ = 2L/N.

Adjacency Matrix A ∈ {0,1}^{N×N}:

A_ij = 1  if edge (i,j) ∈ E
A_ij = 0  otherwise
  • Undirected networks: A is symmetric (A_ij = A_ji), since an edge between i and j is the same as one between j and i. Directed networks have non-symmetric adjacency matrices. Memory cost is O(N²) regardless.
  • No self-loops: The diagonal entries A_ii = 0 by convention, as a node is not considered its own neighbor (unless self-loops are explicitly modeled).
  • Powers of A: The entry (A²)_ij counts the number of walks of length 2 from node i to node j — in general (Aᵏ)_ij counts all walks of length k. This connects graph topology directly to linear algebra and enables efficient computation of path-based measures.

Clustering Coefficient: C_i = 2·t_i / (k_i(k_i − 1)) — fraction of node i's neighbors that are connected to each other.

Average path length: L = 1/[n(n−1)] Σ_{i≠j} d(i,j). Diameter D = max path length.

Degree Distribution P(k)

P(k) = fraction of nodes with degree k — the "fingerprint" of a network.

  • Erdős-Rényi (ER) G(N,p): P(k) follows Poisson distribution. No hubs, exponential tail.
  • Scale-free (BA): P(k) ~ k^{−γ} (power law, γ ≈ 2–3). Heavy tail → hubs. Examples: WWW, airports, proteins.

Small-World Networks (Watts-Strogatz, 1998)

Real networks have TWO properties: high clustering C (like a lattice) + short average path length L ~ log N (like a random graph).

WS model: Start with ring lattice → rewire each edge with probability p → small p adds "shortcuts" that dramatically reduce L while keeping C high.

Key Result
"Six degrees of separation": in social networks, any two people are connected by ~6 hops.

Q 5
Building a graph with a computer. Types of graphs. Propose a dynamical model on a complex network.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
To study a network on a computer you first have to grow one. Three classic recipes give three personalities: pure randomness (Erdős–Rényi), random plus a few shortcuts (Watts–Strogatz small-world), and "rich get richer" (Barabási–Albert scale-free, with hubs). Then you run a process — say an epidemic — on top and watch how the wiring shapes the outcome.

Data Structures

  • Adjacency matrix: N×N array A[i][j]=1 if edge exists. Fast lookup O(1), memory O(N²) — bad for sparse networks.
  • Adjacency list: list of neighbors for each node. Memory O(N+L) — efficient for sparse networks. Standard for large networks.

Graph Generation Models

Erdős-Rényi G(N,p):

1. Start with N isolated nodes
2. For each pair (i,j): draw r ~ U[0,1]
   if r < p: add edge (i,j)

Properties: Poisson degree distribution, ⟨k⟩ = (N−1)p. Giant component emerges at p > 1/N (phase transition). Low clustering, short paths.

Watts-Strogatz Small-World:

1. Create ring lattice: N nodes, each connected to k nearest neighbors
2. For each edge: with probability p, rewire to random node

Tuning p: regular (p=0) → small-world → random (p=1). High clustering + short paths at intermediate p.

Barabási-Albert Scale-Free (Preferential Attachment):

1. Start with m₀ fully connected nodes
2. At each step: add new node with m edges
3. Connect to existing node i with probability:
   Π(i) = k_i / Σ_j k_j   ("rich get richer")

Results in power-law degree distribution P(k) ~ k^{−3}. Natural model for WWW, citations, social networks.

Dynamical Model: SIR Epidemic on a Network

Each node = one person. Each edge = potential transmission route. States: S, I, R.

Rules (discrete time): Each infected node I infects each susceptible neighbor S with probability β. Each I recovers with probability γ.

Basic reproduction number: R₀ = β · ⟨k⟩ / γ. If R₀ > 1: epidemic spreads.

Key Result: Scale-Free Networks
On scale-free (BA) networks: NO epidemic threshold! Even tiny β causes spreading because hubs act as super-spreaders. Threshold = ⟨k⟩/⟨k²⟩ → 0 as ⟨k²⟩ → ∞.
for infected_node in I_list:
    for neighbor in adjacency_list[infected_node]:
        if state[neighbor] == 'S' and random() < beta:
            state[neighbor] = 'I'   # infection
    if random() < gamma:
        state[infected_node] = 'R'  # recovery

Q 6
What is the Lattice Boltzmann Method? Purpose, equations, algorithm.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Instead of solving the hard fluid equations directly, LBM pretends a fluid is swarms of fictitious particles hopping between grid points along a few fixed directions. Each step is just "collide locally, then stream to your neighbor." Do this everywhere and real fluid behaviour (Navier–Stokes) emerges — and it's trivially parallel and great for messy geometries.

Purpose

The Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) is a numerical method for simulating fluid dynamics (solving Navier-Stokes equations) using a mesoscopic kinetic approach. Advantages: naturally parallel, easy complex geometries, simple to implement, handles multi-phase flows.

Physical Foundation: Kinetic Theory

Instead of tracking individual molecules, LBM tracks the probability density function f(x, v, t) — density of particles at position x, velocity v, time t. Boltzmann equation: ∂f/∂t + v·∇f = Ω(f).

Macroscopic variables from moments of f:

Density:   ρ = ∫f dv
Momentum:  ρu = ∫v·f dv

D2Q9 Discretization

Space → regular lattice. Velocity space → 9 discrete directions in 2D:

D2Q9: 9 velocity directions
   6  2  5
   3  0  1     e₀=(0,0), e₁=(1,0), e₂=(0,1), e₃=(-1,0), e₄=(0,-1)
   7  4  8     e₅=(1,1), e₆=(-1,1), e₇=(-1,-1), e₈=(1,-1)

At each node: 9 populations f_i(x,t). Memory: N × M × 9 floats.

Macroscopic Variables

ρ(x,t)  = Σᵢ fᵢ(x,t)          (density)
ρu(x,t) = Σᵢ fᵢ(x,t)·eᵢ       (momentum)

BGK Collision Equation

fᵢ(x + eᵢδt, t + δt) = fᵢ(x,t) - (1/τ)[fᵢ(x,t) - fᵢᵉq(x,t)]

τ = relaxation time. Viscosity: ν = cs²(τ − 0.5)δt. cs = 1/√3 (speed of sound on lattice).

Equilibrium distribution:

fᵢᵉq(ρ, u) = wᵢ · ρ · [1 + (eᵢ·u)/cs² + (eᵢ·u)²/(2cs⁴) - u²/(2cs²)]
Weights: w₀=4/9, w₁₋₄=1/9, w₅₋₈=1/36

Algorithm: Two Steps Per Iteration

COLLISION (local, at each node — fully parallel):

f̃ᵢ(x,t) = fᵢ(x,t) - (1/τ)[fᵢ(x,t) - fᵢᵉq(ρ(x,t), u(x,t))]

STREAMING (propagate to neighbors):

fᵢ(x + eᵢδt, t + δt) = f̃ᵢ(x,t)

Q 7
Computational Fluid Dynamics. Lattice Gas vs Lattice Boltzmann. Differences.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
CFD = computing how fluids flow. Two "bottom-up" lattice ways to do it: lattice-gas uses literal yes/no particles (simple but noisy), while lattice-Boltzmann tracks the smooth average number of particles (real numbers, far less noise). Same spirit — collide and stream — but LBM trades Boolean grit for smoothness.

Navier-Stokes Equations

∂ₜu + (u·∇)u = -(1/ρ₀)∇p + ν∆u    (momentum)
∇·u = 0                               (incompressibility)

Terms: ∂ₜu = local acceleration; (u·∇)u = convective (nonlinear!); −∇p/ρ = pressure gradient; ν∆u = viscous dissipation.

Reynolds number: Re = UL/ν. Low Re → laminar; High Re → turbulent. N-S are nonlinear PDEs → numerical simulation essential.

Lattice Gas Automata (LGA) — Historical Predecessor

HPP model (1973): square lattice. FHP model (1986): hexagonal lattice.

  • Each particle: Boolean variable — present (1) or absent (0)
  • Each step: Streaming (move to neighbor) + Collision (scatter conserving mass + momentum)

Problems with LGA — despite the elegance of the approach, lattice gas automata suffer from three fundamental limitations that motivated the development of LBM:

  1. Statistical noise: Because each particle is represented as a Boolean (present or absent), the density and momentum fields computed from local averages have large random fluctuations. Extracting smooth macroscopic fields requires ensemble averaging or spatial smoothing over many runs, which is computationally expensive and defeats much of the simplicity advantage.
  2. Galilean invariance violated: The advection term (u·∇)u in the Navier-Stokes equation is not correctly recovered by LGA on standard lattices. The recovered equations contain a spurious density-dependent factor in the convective term, meaning the model does not correctly describe flows at different mean velocities — a fundamental physical symmetry is broken.
  3. Dimension constraints: The hexagonal FHP model works correctly in 2D, but extension to 3D requires more complex lattice geometries (face-centered hypercubic, FCHC) with many velocity directions, making 3D LGA practically very difficult to implement and use.

💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Computing every pair of gravitational pulls among N bodies costs N² — hopeless for millions of stars. Barnes–Hut uses the trick your eye already uses: a distant galaxy looks like one blurry blob, so lump far-away groups into a single effective mass. A tree of nested boxes makes this lumping systematic, cutting the cost to N log N.

The Problem: N-body

N particles, each with mass mᵢ and position xᵢ. Force: F_ij = G·mᵢ·mⱼ / |xᵢ − xⱼ|².

Naive: O(N²). N = 10⁶ → 10¹² operations → impractical.

Key Insight

Distant clusters of particles can be approximated as a single particle at the cluster's center of mass with the total mass.

θ-criterion: if s/d < θ → treat cluster as single particle (where s = bounding box size, d = distance to cluster center of mass, θ ≈ 0.5–1.0). Smaller θ = more accurate but slower.

- Recursively subdivide into quadrants
- Each node stores: total mass M, center of mass X_com, bounding box
- Subdivide until each leaf has ≤ 1 particle

Q 9
Three inter-particle potentials (Gravity, Coulomb, Lennard-Jones). Algorithmic complexity and workarounds.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Three forces, three complexity stories. Gravity and Coulomb are long-range (everything pulls on everything → expensive, needs tree / FMM / Ewald tricks), while Lennard-Jones dies off fast (so just ignore far neighbors with a cutoff). The reach of the potential decides the algorithm.

Gravity

V_grav(r) = −G·m₁·m₂ / r     F = G·m₁·m₂ / r²

Gravity is a long-range potential that decays only as 1/r, meaning every particle in the system exerts a non-negligible force on every other particle regardless of separation. It is always attractive — there is no gravitational repulsion. As a result, the naive all-pairs computation (evaluating every pair (i, j) separately) scales as O(N²). For N = 10⁶ particles this requires 10¹² force evaluations — completely intractable. Several algorithms exploit the smoothness of the gravitational field at large distances to reduce this cost:

  • Barnes-Hut: Recursively groups distant particles into clusters and approximates each cluster as a single point mass at its center of mass, achieving O(N log N) time. The θ-criterion (s/d < θ) controls the accuracy-speed trade-off (see Q8 for full details).
  • Fast Multipole Method (FMM): Uses a hierarchical multipole expansion to compute all gravitational interactions to arbitrary precision in O(N) time — the theoretically optimal scaling, though with a larger constant factor than Barnes-Hut.
  • Particle-Mesh: Projects masses onto a regular grid, solves Poisson's equation for the potential via FFT, and interpolates forces back to particle positions. Also O(N log N) but with excellent practical performance on uniform distributions.

Coulomb (Electrostatic)

V_Coulomb(r) = k·q₁·q₂ / r     F = k·q₁·q₂ / r²

The Coulomb potential has exactly the same 1/r form as gravity, making it equally long-range — every charge interacts with every other charge in the system. The critical difference is that Coulomb forces can be both attractive (opposite charges: q₁q₂ < 0) and repulsive (like charges: q₁q₂ > 0). The naive all-pairs computation again scales as O(N²). For large molecular dynamics simulations, the same tree-based algorithms apply, plus one additional technique that is standard practice:

  • Barnes-Hut and FMM: Directly applicable here as for gravity, grouping distant charge clusters into multipole approximations of the electrostatic potential.
  • Ewald Summation: The go-to method for molecular dynamics with periodic boundary conditions. The conditionally convergent long-range sum is split into two absolutely convergent parts: a short-range contribution computed directly in real space (decays fast due to a Gaussian screening function), and a long-range contribution computed efficiently in Fourier (reciprocal) space using FFT. This decomposition makes periodic electrostatics tractable and is the standard technique implemented in packages such as GROMACS and NAMD for biomolecular simulations.

Lennard-Jones

V_LJ(r) = 4ε [(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶]
  • ε = depth of potential well; σ = particle diameter
  • (σ/r)¹² = repulsive (Pauli exclusion, very short-range)
  • (σ/r)⁶ = attractive (Van der Waals)
  • r = 2^{1/6}σ: equilibrium (energy minimum)
Key Difference
LJ is short-range — decays as r⁻⁶. At r > 2.5σ, potential is negligible (<1.6% of ε). This enables cutoff-based O(N) algorithms!
  • Cutoff radius r_c = 2.5σ: simply ignore all pairs where r > r_c
  • Verlet neighbor list: pre-compute neighbors within r_c + r_skin, reuse for multiple steps
  • Linked-cell method: divide box into cells of size ≥ r_c; only check adjacent cells (27 in 3D) → O(N)

What are agent-based models? Agent types, behavior, implementations, examples.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Agent-based modeling says: don't write one equation for the whole crowd — give each individual simple rules and let the crowd's behavior emerge. From dumb "if-then" reflex agents up to goal-seeking optimizers, the global pattern (a traffic jam, a flock of birds) is never written down anywhere — it appears.

What is an ABM?

An Agent-Based Model simulates a system as a collection of autonomous agents interacting with each other and their environment according to local rules. Fundamental principle: emergence — complex global patterns from simple local interactions. No central controller.

  • Autonomy: each agent acts independently
  • Heterogeneity: agents can have different properties
  • Local interactions: agents only interact with neighbors
  • Emergence: macro-level patterns not explicitly programmed

Agent Behavior Loop

At each time step, every agent executes the same three-phase cycle:

At each time step:
  1. PERCEIVE: observe environment (neighbors' states)
  2. DECIDE: apply decision rule
  3. ACT: update state, move, communicate

The PERCEIVE phase restricts each agent to local information only — typically the states of nearby neighbors rather than the global configuration. This constraint is what makes ABMs genuinely bottom-up: no agent can "see" the emergent pattern they collectively create. The DECIDE phase applies the agent's rule or policy to the perceived information (this can be a simple if-then rule, or a utility-maximizing calculation). The ACT phase carries out the chosen action, which may change the agent's own state, its position in space, or the states of neighbors through communication or physical contact.

Update Scheduling

A critical and often overlooked design choice in ABMs is the update schedule — the order in which agents are processed within each time step. This seemingly minor detail can qualitatively change the model's outcomes:

  • Synchronous (parallel): All agents perceive the world based on the previous time step's states, make their decisions simultaneously, and all act at once. Well-defined, reproducible, and easy to analyze mathematically — but can produce artifacts (e.g., two agents simultaneously deciding to occupy the same cell).
  • Asynchronous (random sequential): At each time step, one agent is selected at random and fully updated (perceive → decide → act) before the next agent is selected. Repeated N times so that on average each agent acts once per step. More biologically and socially realistic since interactions happen one at a time — but results can differ substantially from the synchronous case, and the random order introduces stochasticity even in otherwise deterministic rules.

Ant Colony Optimization: Ants deposit pheromone on path to food. Others follow gradients. Pheromone evaporates. Short paths accumulate more → positive feedback → optimal path emerges.

Q 11
Agent model spatial aspects: topologies, neighborhoods. Different approaches and consequences.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
In an agent model, "where you are" decides "who you interact with." Whether agents live on a grid, in continuous space, on a network, or in a well-mixed soup — and whether a neighborhood is 4 cells or 8 — quietly but decisively changes the emergent outcome.

Why Topology Matters

The topology (how agents are connected or arranged) fundamentally shapes which agents interact, how states propagate, the patterns that emerge, and speed of convergence. Same local rules + different topology = different emergent behaviors.

Space Representations

How you represent space is one of the most consequential modeling decisions in an ABM — it determines which agents can interact, how information and states propagate, and what kinds of patterns can emerge:

  • Regular Grid (Lattice): Space is divided into a discrete 2D grid of cells (square, hexagonal, or triangular lattice). Each agent occupies one cell and can only interact with agents in neighboring cells. Simple to implement and well-studied mathematically. The geometry of the lattice affects spreading patterns — a square grid produces anisotropic (diamond-shaped) fronts with Von Neumann neighborhoods.
  • Continuous Space: Agents have real-valued positions (x, y) ∈ ℝ² and interact with all other agents within a specified Euclidean radius r. This is more physically realistic and allows smooth, density-varying distributions. Used in Boids (flocking), particle simulations, and predator-prey models with explicit movement.
  • Network Topology: There is no geometric space at all — agents are nodes in a graph and can only interact with their direct graph neighbors (connected by edges). This is the correct representation for social dynamics where "who you interact with" is determined by social ties, not physical proximity. Examples: opinion dynamics, disease spreading on a contact network (ER, WS, or BA).
  • No Space (Mean-Field): Every agent interacts equally with every other, as if all are uniformly mixed in a single container. This corresponds to the ODE limit (e.g., the SIR compartmental model). Analytically tractable and yields clean equations, but ignores all spatial structure, clustering, and local correlations — often the least realistic assumption.

Boundary Conditions

What happens at the edges of a finite simulation domain is a modeling choice that can significantly affect results, especially for small grids where edge agents make up a large fraction of the population:

  • Periodic (toroidal): The left edge is connected to the right edge, and the top to the bottom, forming a torus. An agent leaving one side immediately re-enters from the opposite side. This effectively simulates an infinite or periodically tiled system, eliminating artificial edge effects. The most common choice in ABM and lattice simulations.
  • Fixed/Walled: The grid has hard boundaries that agents cannot cross. Agents at the edges have fewer neighbors than interior agents (corner agents may have only 2 neighbors instead of 8 in a Moore neighborhood). This introduces boundary effects that can distort results for small systems, but is appropriate when the walls have physical meaning (e.g., the walls of a room, the border of a city).
  • Reflective: Agents or signals that reach the boundary are reflected back symmetrically, as if by a mirror. Used in some physical simulations where the boundary represents a repulsive wall or a symmetry plane. Equivalent to extending the domain by mirroring it at each boundary.

Q 12
Challenges of modeling social systems. Schelling model. Opinion dynamics on networks.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Modeling people is uniquely hard — they react to the model, they think, they change the rules. Schelling's stunning lesson: even when everyone is perfectly happy to be a minority, mild preferences cascade into near-total segregation. Micro tolerance ≠ macro mixing.

Challenges of Modeling Social Systems

Social systems differ fundamentally from natural (physical) systems:

  • Reflexivity: Agents KNOW they are being modeled → they react to the model. A weather model doesn't change the weather; an economic model can change markets.
  • Non-stationarity: Rules change over time. Norms, institutions, laws evolve. (Gravity is always F=Gm₁m₂/r².)
  • Heterogeneity + Cognition: Humans have beliefs, preferences, bounded rationality — not interchangeable particles.
  • Memory & Learning: Past experiences shape present behavior; path dependency matters.
  • Feedback Loops: Individual behavior shapes social structures; structures constrain behavior → tipping points, cascades, lock-ins.
  • No Closed-Form Equation: Simulation is necessary to test mechanisms.

Schelling Spatial Segregation Model (1971)

Thomas Schelling (Nobel 2005): Can mild preferences for similar neighbors generate strong residential segregation?

Setup: 2D grid (e.g., 50×50). Two types: Red and Blue + empty cells. Each agent has tolerance threshold τ ∈ [0,1].

Local rule: (1) Pick agent at random. (2) Count fraction f_s of same-type neighbors. (3) If f_s < τ → unhappy → moves to random empty cell. (4) Else → stays.

Results (tipping points):

  • τ ≤ 0.25: well-mixed equilibrium
  • 0.30 ≤ τ ≤ 0.50: clear segregated clusters emerge spontaneously
  • τ ≥ 0.75: no equilibrium (everyone always unhappy)
The Surprise
Agents who are happy with 30% same-type neighbors end up in neighborhoods that are 70–80% same-type! Each move destabilizes formerly happy neighbors → cascade. Tiny biases → large effects. Micro ≠ Macro.

Opinion Dynamics

Voter Model (discrete opinions {0,1}): Pick random agent i → i adopts opinion of a random neighbor j. No threshold. Consensus in O(N) steps on complete graph; O(N²) on regular lattice; faster on scale-free (hubs lock in).

Deffuant (Bounded Confidence): Opinions xᵢ ∈ [0,1]. Agents i, j interact only if |xᵢ − xⱼ| < ε. Update: xᵢ ← xᵢ + μ(xⱼ − xᵢ). Small ε → many clusters (fragmentation); large ε → consensus. Models echo chambers.

Q 13
Define a cellular automaton (CA) intuitively and mathematically. What is the goal of this approach? How is the evolution computed, and how many CA can exist?
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
A cellular automaton is a fictitious universe where everything is discrete: space is a grid of cells, time ticks in steps, and each cell holds one of a few states. Every cell looks only at its neighbors and updates by the same simple rule, all at once. The magic — and the whole point — is that dead-simple local rules produce astonishingly complex global behavior (snowflakes, gliders, even a universal computer). It's the cleanest demonstration of emergence.

Intuitive Definition

Imagine an infinite chessboard. Each square (cell) is colored from a small palette (the states). At every clock tick, each square recolors itself based only on the colors of its immediate neighbors, following one fixed rule used by every cell. Run it forward and watch patterns grow. That is a CA: a discrete model of reality in space, time, and state.

Mathematical Definition — the tuple ⟨A, S, 𝒩, Φ⟩

  • A — the cellular space: a regular lattice of cells/sites in d dimensions (1D line, 2D square/hexagonal grid, …).
  • S — a finite set of possible states for each cell (e.g., {0,1}; dead/alive).
  • 𝒩 — the neighborhood: the fixed set of cells whose states determine the update.
  • Φ — the local, homogeneous evolution rule: Φ : S|𝒩| → S, the same for every cell and every time.

Update is synchronous (parallel): all cells compute their next state from the current configuration simultaneously. For a 1D CA: si(t+1) = Φ(si−1(t), si(t), si+1(t)).

Neighborhoods & Boundary Conditions

  • Neighborhoods (2D): von Neumann (4 orthogonal neighbors), Moore (8, including diagonals), Margolus (2×2 blocks, used for reversible/momentum-conserving rules).
  • Boundary conditions (a finite grid needs them): periodic (wrap-around torus), fixed (constant border value), adiabatic (copy the edge cell), reflexive (mirror). The initial condition must also be specified.

Goal of the Approach

CA are a modeling framework AND a new paradigm for computation. The deep idea (§ "CA as an abstraction of reality"): nature has macro/meso/micro levels, and macroscopic behavior depends very little on microscopic detail — only symmetries and conservation laws survive. So instead of simulating true microphysics, we invent a fictitious microworld with its own simple "microscopy" that is easy to run on a (parallel) computer yet reproduces the desired macroscopic behavior. CA are simple, flexible, intuitive, efficient. They also illuminate links between complex systems, universal computation, algorithmic complexity and intractability.

Computing the Evolution (algorithm)

  1. Store the configuration in an array; use two buffers (read from the old, write to the new) because the update is synchronous — overwriting in place would corrupt neighbors.
  2. For each cell, read its neighborhood and apply Φ. Two ways to evaluate Φ: on-the-fly computation (e.g. parity rule sij(t+1) = [si−1,j+si+1,j+si,j−1+si,j+1] mod 2) or a precomputed lookup table indexed by the neighborhood configuration (fast, rule-agnostic).
  3. Apply boundary conditions at the edges; swap buffers; repeat.

Generalizations: stochastic CA (apply rule Φ₁ with prob p, Φ₂ with 1−p), asynchronous update (sequential — loses parallelism but avoids spurious oscillations), non-uniform CA (rule varies in space). Each can be re-expressed as a standard CA with an extra bit field.

How Many CA Exist? (counting rules)

Let m = number of states per cell and k = number of neighbors. The neighborhood can be in mk configurations; the rule assigns one of m output states to each configuration, so:

Number of possible rules = m(mk)

Elementary CA (1D, m=2 states, k=3 neighbors): 2(2³) = 2⁸ = 256 rules — Wolfram's famous numbered rules (rule 30, 90, 110, 184…). The number explodes super-exponentially, so the space of "possible universes" is finite but astronomically large.

Key Examples

  • Parity rule: sum of 4 neighbors mod 2 → complex patterns from a single seed.
  • Game of Life (2D, Moore, 0/1): birth if exactly 3 live neighbors, survival with 2–3, else death. Produces gliders, glider guns — and is a universal computer.
  • Traffic, rule 184: ni(t+1) = ni(t)·ni+1(t) + (1−ni(t))·ni−1(t); reproduces the fundamental flow diagram (free flow vs jam).
  • Forest fire, excitable media (Greenberg–Hastings: resting→excited→refractory), snowflake growth (solidify if exactly one solid neighbor), Langton's loops and von Neumann's 29-state self-reproducing automaton (historical origin, 1940s).

Q 14
What is a diffusion process? Explain the different approaches discussed in the course.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
Diffusion is what happens when a crowd of particles jiggles randomly — ink in water, heat in a metal rod, a smell across a room. No single particle wants to go anywhere, yet pure randomness inevitably spreads stuff from where there's a lot to where there's little, smoothing concentration out. The same phenomenon has three faces: a smooth PDE (macro), a random walk (micro), and a lattice-gas (meso) — and the bridge between micro and macro is the Central Limit Theorem.

What is Diffusion?

Diffusion is the net transport of a quantity (mass, heat, concentration) from high to low concentration, driven by random microscopic motion. It is described by:

  • Fick's law: the flux is proportional to the (negative) gradient, J = −D∇ρ (particles flow downhill in concentration).
  • Combined with conservation (∂tρ + ∇·J = 0) this gives the diffusion equation:
∂ρ/∂t = D ∇²ρ   (1D: ∂tρ = D ∂²xρ)

Hallmark behavior: a blob spreads with width ∝ √t, and the mean-square displacement grows linearly, ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt (1D). D is the single parameter linking all descriptions.

Approach 1 — Macroscopic PDE / Finite Differences

Solve the diffusion equation directly on a grid (the "dynamical systems / Q3" toolbox). Explicit forward-time, centered-space scheme:

ρ(t+Δt, x) = ρ(t,x) + (DΔt/Δx²)·[ρ(t,x−1) − 2ρ(t,x) + ρ(t,x+1)]

Stability condition: the scheme is stable only if DΔt/Δx² ≤ 1/2 (otherwise it blows up). ✅ Fast, smooth, deterministic. ❌ Complex geometries and obstacles make the boundary conditions awkward.

Approach 2 — Microscopic Random Walk / Monte-Carlo

Track many independent walkers on a lattice. A particle moves right with prob W+, left with W−, stays with W0. The probability density obeys the master equation:

p(t+1, x) = p(t,x−1)·W₊ + p(t,x)·W₀ + p(t,x+1)·W₋

Matching this to the discretized diffusion equation forces W₊ = W₋ = DΔt/Δx² and W₀ = 1 − 2DΔt/Δx², with the same stability bound DΔt/Δx² ≤ 1/2 (so that W₀ ≥ 0).

🔑 Why micro = macro: the Central Limit Theorem
A walker's position after N random steps is a sum of independent increments → by the CLT its distribution tends to a Gaussian, which is exactly the fundamental solution of the diffusion equation. The macroscopic D emerges from microscopic step statistics: D = (step²)/(2·step-time).

✅ Trivial to add obstacles/complex geometry (just block cells). ❌ Statistical noise — need many walkers; error ~ 1/√N (this is a Monte-Carlo method, Q15).

Approach 3 — Mesoscopic: Lattice Gas / Lattice Boltzmann

Use particle distribution functions fi(x,t) on a lattice with streaming + collision (Q6/Q7). In the macroscopic limit (Chapman–Enskog), a diffusive LB model recovers ∂tρ = D∇²ρ (and advection–diffusion if a flow is added). ✅ Handles complex geometry and couples naturally to fluid flow; intrinsically parallel. ❌ More machinery than a plain random walk.

Approach 4 — Cellular-Automaton Diffusion

A fully discrete rule (e.g. random redistribution among neighbors, or a Margolus block rule) makes "diffusion" emerge from local updates — the CA philosophy (Q13): a fictitious, parallel microworld whose macroscopic limit is the diffusion equation.

Q 15
How are Monte-Carlo simulations used to sample a given probability distribution? Explain the concept of Monte-Carlo to simulate a natural process. Give simple examples.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
When a problem is too hard to solve exactly, throw dice at it. Monte-Carlo replaces "compute the exact answer" with "generate many random scenarios and average them." Like estimating π by throwing darts at a square, or finding the average energy of a gas by visiting representative configurations instead of summing over the impossibly many possible ones.

Definition (course)

A Monte-Carlo simulation generates, through a stochastic process, possible scenarios of evolution of a phenomenon — each with the correct probability of occurrence — so that the phenomenon can be analyzed statistically. It relies on repeated random sampling and is heavily based on random-number generators. (Coined in the 1940s by Ulam, von Neumann and Metropolis on the Manhattan Project; named after the casino.)

Why It Works — Monte-Carlo Integration

We often want an average over a distribution ρ(x):

⟨T⟩ = ∫ T(x) ρ(x) dx  ≈  (1/N) Σi=1..N T(xi)

If we can draw samples xi distributed according to ρ, the simple average estimates the integral. The key theoretical justification: the statistical error decreases like 1/√N independent of the dimension. Grid quadrature costs explode as (points)dimension, so for high-dimensional state spaces (a gas of many particles) Monte-Carlo is the only feasible option.

Sampling a Given Distribution ρ(x)

The challenge: produce points xi that occur with frequency ρ(xi).

  • Direct sampling: invert the cumulative distribution (works only for simple ρ).
  • Rejection sampling: sample uniformly under an envelope, reject points above ρ.
  • Problem: in real systems the state space is so huge that not all x can be enumerated — even if ρ is known we cannot normalize it. We need MCMC.

Markov-Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC)

Build a random walk through state space: a "particle" hops from x to x′ with transition probability Wx→x′. The probability of being at x evolves by the master equation:

p(t+1, x) = Σx′ p(t, x′) Wx′→x

We want the walk's stationary distribution to be ρ: p(t+1,x) = p(t,x) = ρ(x). A sufficient condition is detailed balance:

ρ(x′) Wx′→x = ρ(x) Wx→x′

The Metropolis Rule

A simple solution of detailed balance:

Wx→x′ = min(1, ρ(x′)/ρ(x))

Crucial advantage: only the ratio ρ(x′)/ρ(x) appears — the normalization of ρ is never needed (often impossible to compute). Algorithm: propose x′ at random; accept it if rand() < ρ(x′)/ρ(x) (always accept if it's more probable, sometimes accept downhill moves so the walk explores).

Variants: Glauber rule W = ρ(x′)/(ρ(x)+ρ(x′)); Metropolis–Hastings with a non-uniform proposal sx→x′: W = min(1, ρ(x′)sx′→x / ρ(x)sx→x′).

Simulating a Natural Process — the Ising Model

A magnet = a lattice of spins si ∈ {−1,+1}. Aligned neighbors have lower energy: E = −J Σ⟨ij⟩ sisj. At equilibrium temperature T, statistical mechanics gives the Boltzmann distribution ρ(s) ∝ exp(−E/kBT). To compute the average magnetization we sample ρ:

repeat (Monte-Carlo step = N spin trials):
   pick a spin i at random
   compute ΔE of flipping it (s_i → −s_i)
   accept the flip with prob W given by Metropolis/Glauber

Result: low T → spins align (spontaneous magnetization); high T → disorder (no net magnetization); a phase transition at kBTc/J = 2/ln(1+√2) ≈ 2.269. Note we only ever need ΔE — the ratio, again.

Kinetic / Dynamic Monte-Carlo (Gillespie)

To simulate the time evolution of competing processes with known rates ri: pick which event happens with probability ∝ ri (draw s∈[0,Σri], find the bin), realize it, then advance time by Δt = ln(1/s′)/R (exponential waiting time, R = Σri). Example: chemical reaction A⇌B; MC results fluctuate around the analytic ODE solution and self-average as N grows. (This links Monte-Carlo to discrete events, Q16.)

Simple Examples to Quote

  • Coin tosses: probability of 3 heads in 4 throws — simulate 10⁴ trials, count successes (≈ ¼).
  • The card game "war": average duration has no easy formula → just simulate many games and average.
  • Estimating π: throw darts in a unit square, fraction inside the quarter-circle → π/4.
  • 2D Gaussian: MCMC with Metropolis reproduces a target ρ(x,y) after many steps.

What are discrete-event models? Define the elements of such models and the differences with the continuous-time approach. Illustrate with examples.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
In many systems nothing happens most of the time, then suddenly something does — a customer arrives, two billiard balls collide, a packet hits a router. Rather than tick a clock in tiny fixed steps and ask "anything yet?" at each one, a discrete-event simulation computes exactly when the next interesting thing happens and jumps straight there. Between events the state is known by formula.

A point particle moves at constant velocity in a 16 m box with elastic walls. Two ways to find its position after 3 minutes:

DES is both faster and exact — because between collisions the trajectory is a straight line we can solve analytically (twall = (L − y)/vy, etc.).

Definition

A Discrete-Event Simulation (DES) models a system by jumping from one timed event to the next; events are the moments that cause significant changes to the system state. It contrasts with time-driven simulation (fixed Δt steps).

Requirements for DES to apply:

  1. We can compute the state analytically between two events.
  2. Each event occurs at a specific time t ∈ ℝ⁺.
  3. Only a single event occurs at any given time.

Elements of a Discrete-Event Model

  • Entities i, each with a state si(t); the global state is S(t) = {si(t)}.
  • Events j, each associated with an action aj : S → S that modifies the state. The event is the cause; the action is the effect.
  • Two kinds of events:
    • Endogenous — consequences of the system's own evolution (one event schedules the next).
    • Exogenous — originating from outside; they act as boundary / inlet conditions. Because they don't depend on the state, they can be generated in advance, often at random from an empirical distribution (pseudo-random generator).
  • Simulation clock (current time), an event list (future events), and an end condition (no more events, or t ≥ tmax).

Difference with the Continuous-Time (Time-Driven) Approach

⚠ The terminology trap
Discrete-event systems have CONTINUOUS time (events occur at real-valued instants, the clock jumps by variable amounts). Conversely, "continuous-time models" are usually discretized into fixed time steps Δt on the computer. The names are the opposite of what you'd guess.

DES Algorithm (simplified)

t_current = t0
s_i = s_i(t_current)
while not end_condition(t_current, s_i):
    events = f(s_i)        # compute all possible next events
    e_next = g(events)     # choose the one closest in time
    s_i    = e_next.action(s_i)   # execute its action
    t_current = e_next.t   # JUMP to that time

  • Post office / queue (M/M/1-like): windows in states OPEN/BUSY/CLOSED; customers arrive with a processing duration pj, take a free window or wait in a FIFO queue. Events: Open, Process, Close (endogenous), Arrival (exogenous).
  • Event-driven molecular dynamics: hard spheres — compute the next collision time, jump there (the bouncing-particle idea scaled up).
  • Traffic intersection: light RED/GREEN, cars arriving on a side street (Q17 worked example).
  • Volcano ballistics: projectiles follow analytic parabolas between landing events.
  • Gillespie chemical kinetics: each reaction is an event with an exponential waiting time (the bridge to Monte-Carlo, Q15).

Q 17
Discuss discrete-event systems implementation. Summarize the DES approach, then propose concrete methods to implement them efficiently.
💡 Intuition (the one-sentence story)
A DES is really just a to-do list of future events ordered by time: repeatedly pull off the earliest one, run it, and let it schedule new items. So the entire efficiency question boils down to "which data structure lets me always grab the next event — and insert new ones — fastest?" The answer is a priority queue.

Summary of the DES Approach

(Recap of Q16.) A DES advances by jumping from one timed event to the next; the state is computed analytically between events. Each event has an action that modifies the state; events are endogenous (self-generated) or exogenous (external = boundary conditions). The simulation keeps a clock and a list of future events and runs until an end condition.

The Core Data Structure — the Future-Event List (priority queue)

All future events are stored in a priority queue Q, sorted by event time. The crucial generalization for implementation: an action now reads and modifies the queue too,

aj : (S × Q) → (S × Q)

so an action can insert one or more new events (this is how endogenous events are born) or remove events that are no longer valid. The two operations the engine repeats are therefore extract-minimum-time and insert.

DES Algorithm (with the queue)

t_current = t0
Q = exogenous()          # pre-generate all external events into Q
S = initialState(t0)
while not end_condition(t_current, S, Q):
    e_next  = Q.next()            # extract earliest event
    t_current = e_next.time       # jump the clock
    (S, Q)  = e_next.action(S, Q) # run action: may insert/remove events

Cost: the loop runs once per event; total work = (number of events) × (cost of a queue operation). With a good queue this is O(E log E) for E events — versus a naïve unsorted list, which costs O(E²).

Generating Exogenous Events

Because exogenous events (arrivals, boundary inputs) don't depend on the state, they can be generated before the run and inserted into Q. Random inter-arrival times come from inverse-transform sampling: for a Poisson arrival process with rate λ, draw u = rand() and set the waiting time Δt = −ln(u)/λ (an exponential variate). This is the same idea as the Gillespie time step (Q15).

Worked Example — Traffic Intersection

State: side-street light f ∈ {RED, GREEN}, number of waiting cars C. Parameters: a = latency to switch green, b = green time per waiting car. Three event types — CAR (exogenous arrivals), R2G and G2R (endogenous light switches):

event CAR(t):                 event R2G(t):        event G2R(t):
  if C==0 and f=="RED":         f = "GREEN"          f = "RED"
     Q.insert(R2G(t+a))         Q.insert(G2R(t+C*b))
  elif f=="GREEN": pass         C = 0
  else: C = C + 1

A car at a red light schedules a switch-to-green after latency a; turning green schedules the return to red after C·b and clears the count. The engine just keeps pulling the earliest event off Q. (Complications — max green time, pedestrians, light synchronisation — are added as more events/actions.)

Optimization via Monte-Carlo

A DES naturally computes an objective function (e.g. mean waiting-line length), measured each time an event occurs. If exogenous events are random, repeating the run many times gives a Monte-Carlo estimate (Q15) of the objective; sweeping the parameters (a, b, window schedule…) then lets us optimize the system.

Parallel Discrete-Event Simulation

Parallelism is hard because of causality — an event on one processor may invalidate an event already processed on another. Three strategies:

  • Optimistic (e.g. Time Warp): run ahead, and roll back if a late "straggler" event arrives.
  • Pessimistic / conservative: a processor blocks until it is certain no earlier event can arrive.
  • Careless: ignore the occasional causality violation (accept some error for speed).
